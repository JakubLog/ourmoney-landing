# How to Choose a Budget App for Couples? A Practical Comparison

Nearly every couple has tried running a shared budget. Nearly every couple abandoned it in month two or three. Rarely because the tool was bad. Usually because it was designed for one person and two people were supposed to use it.

Here are the four common options and what actually decides between them.

## The spreadsheet

The most common starting point and the most common failure. A spreadsheet is free, flexible and lets you model any scenario. For planning, it is excellent.

For daily use it loses. Nobody opens a spreadsheet in a supermarket queue to log 87 zł of groceries. So expenses get entered once a week from memory, and memory underestimates. After a month the spreadsheet describes a budget, just not yours.

There is a second problem. Almost always one person maintains it, and the other stops opening it within two weeks.

**Good for:** a one off calculation of how to split expenses and a yearly plan. Not for daily tracking.

## Splitwise and settlement apps

Apps like Splitwise do one thing very well: they track who owes whom. For a trip with friends or a flatshare, that is exactly right.

For a couple running a household, it is a different tool than a budget. Splitwise answers "how much will you pay me back", not "how much can we still spend this month". There are no category limits, no monthly budget, no savings goals. There is a running debt balance between two people, which inside a relationship is a strange thing to look at every day.

**Good for:** flatmates, group trips, and couples who keep finances fully separate and only settle up.

## Your banking app

They keep getting better and they all share one limit that cannot be engineered away: they only know transactions from accounts at that bank. If you bank in two places, and most couples do, each of you sees half the picture.

On top of that, automatic categorisation regularly misfires on transfers and mixed purchases, and a genuine two person budget usually does not exist at all, or reduces to sharing one account.

**Good for:** tracking your own spending if you keep everything in one bank on a joint account. Worth reading first whether [a joint account is actually worth it](/en/blog/joint-account-for-couples-is-it-worth-it).

## A budget app designed for couples

The last category is tools where two people are a design assumption rather than an add on. You each have your own login, you both add expenses, you both see the same budget at the same moment.

The difference is practical, not ideological. When your partner adds 120 zł of groceries, you see the updated limit immediately, with no question asked and no evening report. Most of the reasons couples fight about money, which we cover in [Money Arguments in a Relationship](/en/blog/money-arguments-in-a-relationship), simply stop occurring.

## Seven things worth checking before you choose

1. **Genuine access for two people.** One login with a shared password is not a shared budget. Each of you should see who added a given expense.
2. **A flexible split model.** An app that only splits down the middle rules out every couple with different incomes. The models are in [How to Split Expenses in a Relationship](/en/blog/how-to-split-expenses-in-a-relationship-4-proven-models-for-every-couple) and you can run your own numbers in the [calculator](/en/calculator).
3. **Time to add an expense.** Over ten seconds and a few taps, and nobody keeps doing it past the first week. This one criterion decides everything else.
4. **A monthly budget with limits, not just history.** A list of expenses tells you what happened. A budget tells you what you can still spend, and that is the information that changes decisions.
5. **Savings goals.** If you are saving for something specific, the goal belongs in the same place as the budget, with a visible progress bar.
6. **Local context.** Your own currency without conversion, categories that match how you actually live, your own language.
7. **Works on a phone straight away.** Budgets are maintained in shops and car parks, not at a desk.

## What you do not need

You do not need fifty categories, ten year forecasts or charts nobody reads. Powerful tools like YNAB are great for people who enjoy financial systems. For a couple who mainly want to know how much is left this month, they are oversized and get abandoned fastest.

A better test than a feature list is a simple question: will both of us use this on a tired Thursday.

## Summary

Spreadsheet for planning, Splitwise for settling up with friends, your banking app for your own account, and for a genuinely shared budget a tool that assumes two people from the start. If you are choosing something new, check two things first: whether your partner gets full access, and how many seconds it takes to add an expense. The rest matters less than it looks.
